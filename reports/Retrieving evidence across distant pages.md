The missing capability is usually an evidence-backed identity link; a fluent summary does not supply one.  
Start with coreference plus an entity-to-page index, then test short, separately searchable resolved statements.  
Book-level coreference has relevant results, but default BookNLP misses descriptions such as “the old man.”  
Independent book benchmarks show selective graph gains and weak or inconsistent RAPTOR gains; neither is the first purchase here.  
Repair the gold evidence sets first, then test roughly 200–300 held-out questions across 15–20 documents; five per document cannot settle this.

The ranking below is an engineering recommendation for your constraints, not a published head-to-head win with Gemini Embedding 2. No inspected independent study proves a few-dollar solution for name-to-pronoun evidence separated by 100 printed pages across all your domains.

1. **Verified coreference plus a document-scoped entity-to-page index — first choice.**

   Resolve supported pronouns/descriptions to stable entity IDs, using local discourse windows and explicit shared names to connect windows. Store mention → entity → source page/span records in Postgres; use them as another candidate channel alongside existing hybrid search.

   **Why:** a query naming Arun now has an explicit route to a page saying only “he.” An alias dictionary alone lacks this route. Rank the entity’s pages by the question’s event/property terms so a major character does not occupy every candidate slot. The evidence pages can be 100 pages apart without requiring a single pronoun’s antecedent to be 100 pages back.

   **Proof and limits:** BookNLP links pronouns but defaults to excluding named-character links to common descriptions. `pronominalCorefOnly=False` permits those links, with a conflation warning. This makes default BookNLP an incomplete solution to your exact example. ([BookNLP documentation](https://github.com/booknlp/booknlp#character-name-clustering-and-coreference).) BookCoref’s 2025 whole-book evaluation reports BookNLP at 42.2 CoNLL F1, a silver-trained Longdoc model at 67.0, and its annotation pipeline at 80.5. The pipeline uses Qwen2-7B verification and local-window expansion; experiments used one RTX4090 24GB. However, human gold covers only **three canonical novels**; the 50-book corpus is silver data. Its supplied external character lists violate your own-text restriction. Replacing them with a quoted document registry is an unvalidated adaptation, and coreference F1 is not retrieval recall. ([BookCoref, ACL 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.1197/).)

   **Cost/risk:** one local NLP pass plus selective small-model verification; the lookup channel needs no new embedding calls. Store original offsets, entity/document/version IDs, link evidence and abstention status. A quote proves a phrase occurs, not that two phrases identify the same entity. Wrong merges spread through a cluster; reject ambiguous links and audit linkage precision, recall and unresolved coverage. For reports, scope identity by company/period, patient/cohort, contract party, definition and policy version. Literary coreference scores do not establish transfer to those domains.

2. **Selective standalone statements as a separate search view — second choice.**

   For an accepted identity link, create a short statement such as “Arun sold the farm,” pointing to the unchanged event sentence and the identity evidence. Embed that statement as another pgvector row; preserve the original chunk vector and return original pages.

   **Why:** the query and the later event share an explicit name without forcing broad background into the original chunk vector. Separate rows allow independent weighting, deletion and error analysis. Start with at most one view per affected chunk; preserve negation, dates, uncertainty, attribution, quantities and legal modality.

   **Proof and limits:** Dense X Retrieval supports self-contained proposition indexing and mapping retrieved propositions back to source passages. Its Wikipedia experiments improved GTR average passage Recall@5 from 65.2 to 68.0 across five QA datasets. Its corpus had roughly 6.2 times as many propositions as passages, illustrating the expansion cost. This is author evidence with different models, not long-book pair recall. ([Dense X Retrieval, EMNLP 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.845/).) A 2025 study’s Qwen2.5-7B coreference rewriting improved Stella mean-pooling nDCG@5 from .803 to .818 but changed BGE CLS from .799 to .798. Its short-passage datasets, non-Gemini encoders and 48GB test GPU do not establish your result. ([Coreference rewriting study, appendix B](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.07847v1#A2).)

   **Cost/risk:** generation and embedding scale with accepted views, rather than every possible fact. Do not silently replace raw text. Wrong resolution creates a persuasive false fact; stripped qualifiers are especially dangerous in medical and legal material. Fuse/cap evidence by source page so extra vectors do not crowd out the second page or buy an unfair candidate budget. Answers must be supported by original evidence, not the generated statement.

3. **One bounded follow-up search for missing evidence — cheap query-time experiment.**

   Retrieve normally, identify which requested event/relation remains unsupported, then make one or two focused searches using only verified aliases and retrieved bridge information. Merge into the same fixed final page/token budget.

   **Why:** a question asking about two events often ranks the easier event repeatedly. Searching explicitly for the missing event diversifies evidence without summarizing the whole corpus in advance. It combines well with method 1; it cannot invent an identity link absent from the index and retrieved text.

   **Proof and limits:** an independent RAG/GraphRAG comparison finds iterative retrieval and reranking useful across multi-hop settings. That supports this as a control against expensive ingestion, not as proof that a 2B model resolves long-book identities. ([Han et al., RAG vs GraphRAG](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11371v3).)

   **Cost/risk:** one or two extra query embeddings/searches and, if needed, short local-model calls. Keep answer generation out of this first retrieval experiment. An incorrect first hit can send subsequent searches down the wrong path; retain original candidates, cap rounds and forbid guessed aliases. Compare with deeper ordinary retrieval at the same search/scoring budget.

4. **A small entity–passage graph, inspired by HippoRAG2 — conditional escalation.**

   If failures require paths across several entities or relations, add verified adjacency records and bounded traversal or personalized PageRank. Keep original passages and dense retrieval as candidates; PostgreSQL can hold these links without changing your database stack.

   **Why:** an entity lookup handles “same person”; a graph can additionally connect that person to another entity, event or defined term. Graph traversal still depends on correct upstream identity links.

   **Proof and limits:** HippoRAG2 retains passage nodes and retrieves source passages, making it a closer fit than summary-only routing. Its published extraction/filtering stack uses stronger models, so replacing them with local 7B is experimental. Its author comparison reports substantially less extraction text than LightRAG or Microsoft GraphRAG, but does not establish your dollar cost. ([HippoRAG2, ICML 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.14802v1).) Independent GraphRAG-Bench results on novels show complex-question accuracy of 53.38 for HippoRAG2 versus 42.93 for reranked RAG, while factual accuracy is 60.14 versus 60.92. Thus gains are task-specific. The benchmark includes medical guidelines, but generated questions and semantic/LLM evaluation do not measure your exact page-pair recall. ([GraphRAG-Bench, revised 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05690v3).)

   **Cost/risk:** full GraphRAG adds entity/relation extraction, community construction and community summaries; LightRAG adds entity/relation profiles and dual retrieval. These create more generated intermediates than a glossary already exhibiting unreliable entries. Neither supplies proven pronoun resolution automatically. Their original methods target broader retrieval/synthesis, not this specific defect. ([GraphRAG](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.16130v1), [LightRAG](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.05779v1).) LinearRAG avoids LLM relation extraction using entity/sentence/passage links and vectors; it is cheaper design inspiration, with author evidence and no established pronoun solution. Additional sentence/entity vectors still cost storage and API calls. ([LinearRAG](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10114v4).)

5. **RAPTOR or chapter-summary routing — defer for this problem.**

   Build a hierarchy of clustered summaries, search summaries and leaves, then retrieve exact descendant source evidence. This fits broad cross-chapter themes better than minor events hidden behind pronouns.

   **Why/proof:** RAPTOR has real author-reported book/script and scientific-paper QA results on NarrativeQA and QASPER. These establish that hierarchical summaries can help some tasks, not that they preserve exact distant pages. ([RAPTOR, ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.18059v1).) In an independent NovelQA comparison, overall accuracy was 57.12 for both ordinary RAG and RAPTOR; multi-hop accuracy was 47.34 and 48.17 respectively. That weak gain is a poor reason to prioritize a full hierarchy here. ([Independent comparison](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11371v3).)

   **Cost/risk:** additional local summarization and embeddings at each level; summary errors propagate and low-salience details disappear. Every summary needs descendant span/page links, and retrieving a summary covering 50 pages cannot count as finding the necessary page. A cheap chapter router is worth testing later for overview questions, after identity retrieval works.

**Why your notes can add little, and when they help.** A restatement adds no source evidence. A strong embedding model can already represent much of its meaning; any benefit must come from better discrimination or supplied context. A name list offers candidate identities, not the binding between this “he” and Arun. Shared titles/synopses also provide little discrimination among pages of the same book. Context contamination, where a neighbour’s true name or number becomes attached to the wrong chunk, creates false retrieval cues. These are explanations consistent with your results, not a demonstrated causal finding about Gemini.

There is a useful domain distinction. Independent legal research found that short whole-document fingerprints reduced wrong-document retrieval among similar contracts and policies, using a different generator/encoder stack. That addresses “which contract?”, whereas your target often asks “which page inside this book?” ([NLLP 2025 legal retrieval study](https://aclanthology.org/2025.nllp-1.3/).) A 2026 independent study found late/contextualized chunking helped several cross-document tasks but hurt same-book GutenQA retrieval. It did not test generated prefixes or Gemini, so its context-dilution explanation is supporting evidence, not direct replication. ([Beyond Chunk-Then-Embed](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16974v1).) Anthropic’s reported 67% reduction concerns relative top-20 retrieval failure in its own whole-document-context, BM25 and reranking setup; it is not an expected gain for your pipeline. ([Original contextual retrieval experiment](https://www.anthropic.com/research/contextual-retrieval).)

The repository supplies another separation: the hosted reranker scores the first 1,000 characters of **raw child text**, before page/parent hydration, omitting the note. But generic context-aware reranking was already tested with no clear gain, so merely showing existing notes to the scorer is not a new recommendation. Test a verified identity annotation separately and inspect candidate coverage before and after scoring. ([Scorer](/Users/proximus/Documents/antigravity/RAG/src/deep_context/retrieval/ecohash_reranker.py:45), [existing experiment](/Users/proximus/Documents/antigravity/RAG/eval/variant_b/FINDINGS_WHY_LOWER.md:280).)

**Budget and compatibility.** Google's standard Gemini API text embedding list price is $0.20 per million tokens: five million newly embedded tokens costs $1 for embeddings alone. Count formatted text, overlap and retries; local generation/GPU rental and evaluation are separate. Reusing original vectors and adding a small identity view minimizes this bill. No measured throughput or GPU bill establishes an all-inclusive few-dollar run. ([Official pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing).) True late chunking needs token-level hidden states before pooling; the stated one-vector API does not expose that operation. A longer text or sliced whole-document vector cannot reproduce it. ([Late Chunking](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04701v3).)

**A fair test should answer a narrower, verifiable question.**

- **Fix gold before model selection.** Current `far_gold.py` selects questions by earliest/latest gold-page separation, without checking necessity or coreference. Some selected question wordings ask a single fact that could recur on several pages; source inspection must decide whether those pages are alternatives. Encode acceptable *complete support sets*, not every occurrence as jointly required. The existing slice therefore diagnoses distant-label coverage, not established identity-dependent multi-hop recall. ([Selection code](/Users/proximus/Documents/antigravity/RAG/eval/far_gold.py:40), [dataset caveat](/Users/proximus/Documents/antigravity/RAG/eval/pdf_sets.py:1).)
- **Construct genuine cases.** Sample documents/positions before inspecting system misses. Find a later event with an unnamed referent, document its identity chain, and ask for a comparison with an earlier event that actually requires both regions. Two annotators verify exact quotations, entity binding, answer and necessity; deleting either region must remove support for the complete answer. Record physical PDF pages, displayed labels, offsets and source hashes. Include defined terms, acronyms, reporting periods and exceptions in reports. Keep one-page pronoun questions as a separate task rather than requiring an unnecessary early page.
- **Freeze and compare.** Use whole-document held-out splits. Compare raw baseline, explicit-alias lookup, resolved-mention lookup, selective separate statements and one follow-up round. First supply human-correct links as an **oracle diagnostic**; if they do not help, inspect event matching and selection before building an automatic resolver. Keep model/dimension, chunks, index settings, hybrid weights and budgets fixed. Include no-reranker and identical-reranker arms, plus a deeper raw-search control at matched cost.
- **Score actual evidence.** Primary metric: complete-evidence@10 over unique original pages, accepting any complete valid support set. Report candidate, post-fusion, post-reranker and final-page coverage, plus the evidence text visible to the reader. Match unique-page, scoring and token budgets; extra index rows must not silently enlarge them. Track one-page regressions and wrong-entity merges alongside gains. A source-correct identity annotation and raw-only scorer are separate ablations.
- **Use enough independent material.** Start with about 50 development questions, then 200–300 frozen questions across 15–20 or more documents, with several documents per claimed domain. This is an engineering study for a substantial improvement, not universal proof. NovelQA and NovelHopQA offer book-question construction/evidence patterns; neither replaces custom verification of 100-page identity cases. ([NovelQA](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.12766), [NovelHopQA](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.1328.pdf).)

Your 0/5 and 2/5 results have exact 95% binomial intervals of approximately **0–52.2%** and **5.3–85.3%**. For paired comparisons, use wins/losses on identical questions: McNemar tests discordant pairs. Illustrative 80%-power planning for a 10-percentage-point improvement needs about 194–234 independent questions if 25–30% of pairs disagree; a 5-point improvement at 30% disagreement needs about 940. These are derived approximations, not RAG benchmark results or guarantees. Four hundred questions estimates one rate to roughly ±5 points near 50%; that does not guarantee detection of a 5-point gain. Cluster effects by document increase requirements. Report paired differences with document-level uncertainty and domain breakdowns; two books cannot establish cross-domain transfer. ([NIST interval methods](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/prc/section2/prc241.htm), [official McNemar documentation](https://www.statsmodels.org/stable/generated/statsmodels.stats.contingency_tables.mcnemar.html).)
